Finding · latency

Speed decides which strategies make money at all

Every strategy has a delay beyond which it loses money. A maker that only joins the best prices stops being profitable at about 6 ms. Inventory-aware GLFT quoting holds out to 411 ms. With a leading signal, the maker survives to 928 ms.

The cause is visible in the order flow. With delay, the maker acts on a book that has already changed. It re-posts stale prices and cancels more, which sends it to the back of each queue. At the back of a queue, the only orders that reach you are informed sweeps.

    Finding · inventory control

    Managing inventory turns a losing maker into a steady one

    At 20 ms latency, quoting the best bid and ask with no inventory control lost money in every one of the 200 test sessions. Nearly half its lots traded against informed traders, and its position swung by ±14 lots. GLFT skews both quotes away from the inventory it holds, and it had no losing session. Adding the lead signal moved the maker's quotes before the informed traders arrived.

    Finding · adverse selection

    Prices move against most fills

    A markout measures what a fill is worth some time after the trade, valued at the mid price. Every maker earns half a tick at the moment of the fill. A minute later, the symmetric maker has lost more than that, because the trades it got were the ones informed traders wanted. The lead-signal maker keeps most of its edge.

      Finding · queues

      The textbook fill model ignores the queue

      Avellaneda–Stoikov and GLFT assume a quote is filled whenever a trade reaches its price, at a rate A·e−κδ calibrated from the tape. A real order waits behind everyone who got there first.

      The engine

      A matching engine built for speed, checked against two others

      Orders match by price, then by arrival time. Fills happen at the resting order's price. Reducing an order's size keeps its place in the queue, and changing its price sends it to the back, as on a real exchange.

      Prices are array indices

      Prices are integer ticks inside a fixed band, so finding a price level is one array lookup instead of a tree search.

      One preallocated pool

      Orders link into their level's queue by index. Adding, cancelling and filling are constant time and allocate no memory once warm.

      Ids carry a generation

      An order id holds its pool slot plus a counter, so lookups need no hash map, and a stale id can never cancel the new order that reused its slot.

      A two-level bitmap

      When the best level empties, the next one is found 64 levels per instruction using countr_zero, skipping empty words through a summary map.

      How we know it's right

      A deliberately simple reference book, built from std::map and std::deque, shares no code with the fast one. Both books receive the same 800,000 random orders, cancels and size reductions. Every fill (about 171,000) and the full book state must match after every operation. A third book, written in pure Python, checks it again. The tests run under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer on Linux and on a Mac.

      Validation

      The simulation reproduces a published paper

      Before testing anything new, the code reruns the experiment in Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008): 1,000 simulations for each of three risk-aversion settings. Every mean profit lands within 1.3 standard errors of the published tables.

      Method

      Tuned on one set of sessions, tested on another

      • Calibrate volatility and the fill-rate curve from sessions with no market maker, using only the public trade tape.
      • Tune GLFT's risk aversion on 40 training sessions, choosing the highest mean P&L (γ = 80).
      • Test every strategy on 200 sessions it never saw, all on the same random seeds so the comparison is paired.
      • Sweep latency from 0 to 1,000 ms on 100 further unseen sessions.

      An earlier draft chose γ by Sharpe ratio. That kept pushing γ higher, giving up a third of the profit to reduce variance in sessions that never lost money anyway. The chart shows the full trade-off.

      Limitations

      What these results don't show

      • The market is stylized: one-tick spreads, one-tick jumps in value, one fast market maker facing slow background liquidity, and no fees, rebates or competing high-frequency firms. Absolute P&L and Sharpe ratios are not realistic. The comparisons and mechanisms are the result.
      • Time is stylized too. What matters is the maker's latency relative to the informed traders' 200 ms average reaction time.
      • The lead signal is perfect. A real leading market is noisy and partly priced in already, so its 55% gain is an upper bound.
      • Everything comes from one market configuration. Changing the mix of noise and informed traders is the natural next test.
